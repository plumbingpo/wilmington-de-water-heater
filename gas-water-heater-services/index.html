Most gas water heaters in Wilmington, DE work the same basic way. Gas enters through a control valve that doubles as the thermostat and feeds a burner beneath the tank, and the hot exhaust travels up a flue through the middle of the tank and out through the vent. A thermocouple, or a flame sensor on newer electronic models, tells the valve a flame is present; without that signal, the valve shuts off the gas for safety. The design is dependable, but it has a few predictable weak spots. Thermocouples wear out, pilot tubes and burner ports clog, the flame arrestor screen at the base packs up with lint and dust, control valves fail, and vents lose their draft.

Wilmington Water Heater does not guess with gas appliances. We look inside the combustion chamber for scorching, rust, and signs of flame rollout, which tell us whether the flue is blocked or the burner is misbehaving, and we check for carbon monoxide around the heater. One rule comes before all of it: if you smell gas, do not light anything or flip switches. Leave the house, call your gas utility from outside, and then call us once it is safe.

How to Tell Something Is Wrong

A heater that is starting to struggle rarely hides it for long, and the clues are easy to spot once you know them. These are the red flags our technicians see most often before a breakdown:

  • A vent pipe that has separated, rusted, or left scorch marks near the top of the heater
  • A rotten-egg odor near the water heater, in which case get out and call the gas company first
  • Less hot water than the tank should deliver
  • Loud rumbling from the bottom of the tank
  • A pilot light that will not stay lit
  • Soot or scorch marks around the burner access door
  • A cold tank and a burner that will not come on
  • Leaks at the drain valve, the fittings, or the bottom of the tank

What a Trained Technician Brings to the Job

Start with the hazards. Fuel piping, an open flame, and exhaust laced with carbon monoxide come with every gas water heater. A loose flare or threaded joint can leak gas, a vent that is disconnected or poorly sloped can spill exhaust back into the room, and a gas valve installed without a leak test is a risk you cannot see. Then there is the diagnostic side: a pilot that goes out can mean a weak thermocouple, a dirty pilot orifice, a clogged flame arrestor, a draft problem, or a failing gas valve, and working through them by guesswork is slow and costly.

What a trained technician brings is certainty. We test instead of guessing, from thermocouple millivolt readings and burner flame checks to gas pressure at the valve and a draft test at the hood. How to Prevent Water Heater Problems

A few simple habits, like an annual flush and a quick look at the relief valve, can add years to a tank. Put these technician-approved steps to work at home:

  • Install carbon monoxide alarms on every level of the home and test them monthly
  • Keep the area around the heater clear, especially of gasoline, paint, and solvents
  • Keep the thermostat around 120 degrees to save energy and reduce scalding risk
  • Clean dust from around the base so the burner gets plenty of combustion air
  • Check the anode rod every three to five years, since it protects the tank from corrosion
  • Check that the vent connector is secure and rising toward the chimney or flue
  • Drain sediment from the bottom of the tank once or twice a year
